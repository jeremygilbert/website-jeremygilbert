Known Fact

Working in teams mirrors the profession.

From projects like The New York Times’ Snowfall to daily journalism updates it is rare for journalists to work in isolation. Teams make great journalism projects and classrooms are starting to mirrors the profession.

Known Fact

Working in teams drives students’ crazy.

Why? Because they often only do the things that they already know how to do. And the grades for team projects often obscure individual effort.

In the Spring of 2013 Medill students signing up for classes were faced with a choice. Either take interactive Producing and learn about the content aggregation, curation, editing and scripting tools. Or take Advanced Interactive Design and learn about rapid prototyping, wireframing, usability testing and advanced user interface techniques — like responsive design. Both classes relied on a foundation of storytelling and HTML/CSS knowledge. Unfortunately the classes also overlapped in their meeting time. Each class had 18 seats and almost a dozen students wrote trying to figure out which one they should be taking. The right answer was both. After a little bit of bureaucratic maneuvering we merged the classes and got the biggest the room we could get — which had a capacity of thirty-two.

Structure was a critical part of the experience. In figuring out how to merge two different classes it was critical to understand the learning goals of both. To identify overlap and, with concepts specific to one class, what things could be kept and what would be lost. There were three primary areas of agreement between the original two classes: Content, Design and Code. We also plotted out the major deliverables needed to tell the final stories and mapped them to the overall categories. Each of the teams would be responsible for weekly deliverables like: W1: Theme W2: Story Pitch W3: Story Board W4: Draft Content
The primary goal of the class was to empower the students. They got to set their own learning goals, they could pitch workshops and they got to choose the theme of the project and even guide the other project teams’ story choices.
Each student picked from among about twenty-five workshops many offered twice. Some of the workshops required completion of previous workshops as part of sequence. In addition there were a half-dozen workshops that were required for the entire class.
All students had to signup for at least twelve workshops or at most sixteen workshops. They could make changes over the course ... if they got permission from the teammates and approval from their team’s student mentor.
