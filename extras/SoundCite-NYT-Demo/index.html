Worried Egyptians Jam Tahrir Square, but Unity Is Elusive

Moises Saman for The New York Times

Protesters jammed Tahrir Square in Cairo on Friday. Many of them complained about actions of Egypt’s military rulers in recent weeks, as an election approaches.

By LIAM STACK
Published: April 20, 2012

CAIRO — Tens of thousands of Egyptians packed into Tahrir Square in central Cairo on Friday in a spasm of last-minute concern that Egypt’s ruling generals might be trying to sabotage the promised transition to civilian democracy after the presidential election beginning next month.

Tens of thousands gathered in Tahrir Square in Cairo on Friday to protest continued military control of the country ahead of the presidential elections scheduled for May.

The crowd was as large as any that has gathered in the square since the protests that forced out President Hosni Mubarak in February 2011. Even more unusual in the increasingly polarized political climate, Islamists, liberals and leftists all found common ground on at least one front: the demand that the generals, who took power with Mr. Mubarak’s ouster, finally give it up.

The catalysts for the protest were the military-led government’s management of the early stages of the election, and in particular the selection of the candidates. In the past two weeks, Mr. Mubarak’s former spy chief, Omar Suleiman, began a short-lived campaign from inside the office of the intelligence services that set off fears of a plot to restore the old order.

In that same period, a commission of Mubarak-appointed judges unexpectedly blocked Mr. Suleiman and two Islamists from running. They had been considered front-runners for president. And the top military leader suggested that a new constitution should be written and ratified before a handover of power, meaning the military leaders would control that process, too, and have the ability to safeguard their own powers and interests.

“The military council is putting the people in a very hard situation, and people are angry because their demands have not come true,” said Mohamed Hedaya, 19, a student from the countryside who wears the wispy beard of an ultra-conservative Salafi and attended the protest. “People feel like the old regime has not gone anywhere, and under the army we are living with them still.”

Protesters from liberal groups and Islamist movements marched to Tahrir from landmarks across the capital, while others from the provinces beyond arrived in a fleet of chartered buses. Egyptian flags competed for space with the Muslim Brotherhood’s green flag and the black flags of the Salafi movement. A handful of kites in the national colors of red, white and black bobbed in the sky overhead.

“The people will not accept a rigged election,” declared one banner. “The people will not accept a president from the remnants of the old regime.”

Supporters of one of the disqualified candidates, Hazem Salah Abu Ismail, a charismatic ultra-conservative and economic populist, turned out in large numbers to protest a clause in the military’s “constitutional declaration” prohibiting any legal appeal of the decisions of the electoral commission that blocked him from running.

He was knocked off the ballot after it was revealed that his mother was a United States citizen, which automatically disqualified him under provisions of Egyptian law.

His supporters are known for their fierce loyalty, and they have rejected the commission’s decision, arguing it was part of an American conspiracy. “Do you want to be an Americanized nation, or an Islamic nation?” shouted one speaker addressing the crowd at the rally.

“Islamic! Islamic!” They shouted. “Long live Hazem!”

But the issue seemed to resonate beyond Mr. Abu Ismail’s conservative, religious base.

“I am against the military council because the constitutional declaration they made last year was all about rigging the election,” said Abdullah Abdelmeguid, 19, a geophysics student who traveled with friends from a province outside Cairo.

Mr. Abdelmeguid said he planned to vote for Abdel Moneim Aboul Fotouh, a liberal Islamist, or Hamdeen Sabbahi, a longtime activist and nationalist in the tradition of Gamal Abdel Nasser, a former president whose memory still looms large.

“We just want a revolutionary candidate, someone we can support and who stands with the people,” he said.

The anti-Mubarak-era sentiment was echoed in dozens of posters festooned across the square that displayed photographs of politicians running for president who played a role in the Mubarak government: Mr. Suleiman, the former spy chief; Amr Moussa, a former foreign minister and Arab League secretary general; and Ahmed Shafik, a former prime minister.

On the posters, which also showed Field Marshal Mohamed Hussein Tantawi, the head of the ruling military council and Egypt’s current de facto head of state, each man’s face was crossed out in red.

Nabil Osman, a spokesman for the Moussa campaign, called the focus on Mr. Moussa “a smear campaign against non-Islamist candidates.”

He added, “It was unfortunate that today turned into a day of divisive Islamist agendas instead of unity.”

As the sun began to set in Tahrir Square, the outward display of unity once again began to dissolve. It was evident as the crowd quietly self-segregated, with each group gathering around one of 10 stages built overnight, separated by bare patches of wet pavement.

At a stage set up by the Muslim Brotherhood’s political arm, the Freedom and Justice Party, a man in a business suit led a crowd in fist-pumping chants of: “God is great! Long live Egypt!” Behind them a banner read, “Together, defending the revolution.”

Nearby, the rock singer Rami Essam, famous for his acoustic performances in Tahrir Square during the February 2011 uprising, performed.

“We are all one hand, and our demand is just one thing,” he sang. “Leave! Leave!”

The crowd in front of him danced and sang along. “Down, down with military rule,” they sang. “The people want the execution of the field marshal!”

Across the square stood the stage of Mr. Abu Ismail, the disqualified presidential candidate. As Mr. Essam and his fans burst into a chorus of “Freedom! Freedom!,” a speaker on Mr. Abu Ismail’s stage had had enough.

He yelled into the microphone, his voice straining: “Everyone, raise your voices louder, louder! The people want to institute Islamic law! The people want Hazem Salah Abu Ismail!”

Dina Salah Amer contributed reporting.
